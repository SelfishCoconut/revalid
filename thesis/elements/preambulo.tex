\Large{\bft{Abstract}}
\end{center}
\vskip1cm

Penetration testing ends with a report that lists each discovered vulnerability and
the steps needed to reproduce it. Once the development team applies fixes, someone
must confirm that every reported weakness is genuinely closed: the
\emph{revalidation}, or retest, phase. In practice this is done by hand, one finding
at a time, on every remediation cycle: it is repetitive, judgement-laden and poorly
supported by tooling, even as the offensive side of security automates rapidly with
artificial intelligence.

This dissertation presents \emph{revalid}, a local, single-operator system that
automates the revalidation of penetration-test findings while keeping a human in
control of every action that reaches a target. A large language model extracts each
finding and its reproduction steps from the report; a tool-using agent then
re-executes those steps against an authorised target inside an egress-locked sandbox,
proposing each command for the operator's approval before it runs, and returns a
conservative, evidence-linked verdict of \emph{still-open}, \emph{fixed} or
\emph{inconclusive}. The design is deliberately narrow: rather than discovering or
exploiting new vulnerabilities, it specialises in re-verifying reported ones under
per-command human oversight, defaulting to an inconclusive verdict whenever the
evidence is ambiguous. Built on a model-agnostic language-model layer that runs
against either a hosted or a locally hosted model, the system is evaluated
end-to-end on a real penetration-test report against a live, deliberately vulnerable
target. On a locally hosted model it reached the correct verdict on eight of twelve
findings and hedged on the remaining four, never once declaring a still-exploitable
finding fixed, meeting the safety constraint the design exists to enforce while
falling narrowly short of the accuracy target on hedges rather than errors. Its
machine time was comparable to a manual pass over the same findings, and running the same
findings with the approval gate and without it showed that per-command human oversight
costs almost nothing in wall-clock. Those figures were obtained on a small model running
on the operator's own machine, and the reasoning backend is the lever that moves them:
driven by a hosted frontier model, the same system returned the correct verdict on seven
of eight exploitable findings and again issued no false clearance.
