This chapter describes how the project was built rather than what it does. It
presents the development model adopted --- a design-led, AI-assisted single-developer
process (Section~\ref{sec:ai-model}); the sequence of earlier attempts and the
lessons that led to the final approach (Section~\ref{sec:evolution}); the software
process itself, a continuous-flow adaptation of Agile to a solo developer working
with an AI assistant (Section~\ref{sec:process}); the technologies and tooling used
(Section~\ref{sec:tech}); the automated quality gate through which every change must
pass (Section~\ref{sec:ci}); and, as the ESII regulation requires, an explicit
declaration of the use of artificial-intelligence tools
(Section~\ref{sec:ai-declaration}).

\section{A design-led, AI-assisted development model}
\label{sec:ai-model}

The defining methodological choice of this project is the division of labour between
the author and an AI coding assistant. The author acted as \emph{architect and
designer} --- eliciting requirements, deciding the architecture, and reviewing and
validating every output --- while the assistant carried out most of the
implementation under that direction. This inverts the traditional emphasis of a
software project: the scarce effort was spent on design, software-engineering
judgement and architecture, not on typing code.

The evidence for this model is the decision trail. Every significant choice was
recorded as an Architecture Decision Record (ADR) in the lightweight MADR format
\cite{madr, nygard_adr}; the repository contains \textbf{thirty-eight} such records,
each stating a context, the options considered, and the decision with its
consequences. Requirements were captured in a Software Requirements Specification
following the structure of ISO/IEC/IEEE~29148 \cite{iso29148}. These artefacts ---
the ADRs and the requirements specification --- were authored by the developer and
precede the code they govern.

\section{Evolution of the approach}
\label{sec:evolution}

The final method was not the first one tried. It emerged from a series of earlier
projects whose shortcomings shaped it, and the progression is itself instructive.

\subsection{From a pentesting platform to a retesting tool}
\label{subsec:prior-attempts}

The project began under the name \emph{Adeptus} with an ambition that proved too
broad: a full, locally deployable \emph{penetration-testing platform}. Successive
iterations explored this idea --- an AI-powered pentesting web application, and then
a multi-user ``pentest workbench'' pairing each operator with a private AI
conversation over a shared, engagement-scoped knowledge graph, extensible through
external tools. The scope was the problem. Building a general offensive platform
meant most of the effort went into breadth --- orchestration, multi-user concerns,
tool integration --- rather than into a defensible, evaluable research
contribution.

The turning point was narrowing the problem from \emph{pentesting} to
\emph{retesting}. A focused prototype, \emph{Retester}, discarded the platform
ambition and did one thing: analyse a PDF report, extract each finding, and let an
AI agent re-verify it against the audited system in an interactive, steerable
session. That prototype is the conceptual seed of the system presented in this
dissertation. Table~\ref{tab:evolution} summarises the progression and the role each
attempt played.

\subsection{From a hybrid process to disciplined iteration}
\label{subsec:process-evolution}

The way of working evolved in parallel with the scope. The earliest attempts mixed
an up-front, waterfall-style plan with ad-hoc agile execution, and the combination
was unwieldy: a large plan invited large, hard-to-review changes, which is precisely
where AI-assisted development goes wrong. Through trial and error the process
settled on strict iteration --- build a minimal but working end-to-end slice first,
then expand it --- which is the walking-skeleton principle applied throughout this
project.

A second lesson concerned tooling. The workbench iteration of \emph{Adeptus} had
accumulated thirteen custom assistant skills, seven role-playing agents and a
seven-hook automation layer, built for the broad, multi-user platform it was then
pursuing. Once the scope narrowed to a single-operator retesting tool, much of that
apparatus fell outside what the task required. The present project deliberately
\emph{reduced} it to nine task-specific skills, three quality-oriented agents and three
hooks, and made the reduction an explicit, recorded decision (ADR-0004), trading
machinery for discipline.

\section{Software process: Kanban for solo, AI-paced development}
\label{sec:process}

AI-assisted development compresses the cycle time of a feature from weeks to hours,
which makes the fixed-length sprints of Scrum \cite{scrumguide} a poor fit. The
project therefore adopted a continuous-flow \textbf{Kanban} process
\cite{kanban_anderson} on a GitHub Projects board, as an explicit adaptation of the
values of the Agile Manifesto \cite{agilemanifesto} to a single developer working
with an assistant. Work flows continuously from a prioritised backlog; there are no
sprints and no meetings; and \emph{milestones} mark feature-complete increments
rather than time boxes. The project passed through six such milestones, from the M1
walking skeleton to the M6 agentic console; the first four each closed with a
semantically versioned release \cite{semver} (v0.1.0 through v0.4.0), while M5 and
M6 were in progress at the time of writing. Figure~\ref{fig:milestones}
lays out this milestone sequence.

Three rules give the flow its discipline. First, work is \emph{issue-first}: no
feature is written before a board card exists for it, so every change is traceable
to a requirement. Second, every commit follows the Conventional Commits convention
\cite{conventionalcommits}, and every AI-assisted commit carries a
\texttt{Co-Authored-By: Claude} trailer, making AI involvement auditable from the
git history alone. Third, integration is governed by a \emph{CI-gated auto-merge}
(ADR-0003): a pull request merges only once the required checks are green ---
the gate detailed in Section~\ref{sec:ci} --- after which the author reviews it
asynchronously and retains full revert authority. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows
this pipeline.

Two further practices keep an AI-driven codebase healthy over time. The Definition
of Done requires code, tests, docstrings and any affected diagrams to be updated
together; and a longitudinal quality audit, fed by the scheduled metrics workflow of
Section~\ref{sec:ci}, is run before every milestone release to catch the pathologies
specific to AI-assisted development that per-change review cannot see.

\paragraph{Language and project tooling.} The backend is written in Python~3.12, a
high-level, dynamically typed language chosen because the libraries this system
depends on --- the agent framework, and the PDF, HTTP and web libraries --- are
Python-native, and because the language's mature type annotations make the strict
static checking described below possible. Dependencies, virtual environments and the
package build are all managed by \texttt{uv}, a fast Rust-based Python project
manager, adopted because it resolves and installs the full dependency set far faster
than the traditional \texttt{pip}/\texttt{venv} tools, records every version in a
single lockfile so that continuous integration is reproducible, and replaces several
separate utilities with one.

\paragraph{Web service.} The HTTP layer is FastAPI, an asynchronous web framework
that derives request validation and interactive API documentation directly from
Python type hints. It was chosen for that type-first design --- which removes a whole
class of hand-written validation --- and because a single FastAPI process both
exposes the \texttt{/api} backend and serves the compiled front end, exactly what a
single-operator local tool needs. It runs on Uvicorn, the standard ASGI server for
the framework, with the \texttt{websockets} library added so a retest session can
stream the agent's live output to the browser over a WebSocket; report uploads arrive
as multipart form data, parsed by \texttt{python-multipart}.

\paragraph{Data and validation.} Pydantic (v2) is a data-validation library that
enforces typed schemas at runtime from a compiled core. It underpins the system's
``validate before you persist'' rule: every finding the language model returns, and
every request the API receives, is parsed into a Pydantic model, so malformed or
hallucinated data is rejected at the boundary instead of silently corrupting a
verdict; where a contract is expressed as a raw JSON Schema rather than a Python
model, \texttt{jsonschema} checks against it. Durable state --- reports, findings,
retest sessions and their verdicts --- is stored through SQLAlchemy~2.0, an
object-relational mapper, onto SQLite, a zero-configuration database held in a single
file. The pair was chosen so that the operator needs no separate database server,
while the mapper keeps the door open to a heavier database later without rewriting
the data-access code.

\paragraph{Report ingestion.} Penetration-test reports arrive as PDFs written for
human readers. The \texttt{pdfplumber} library extracts their text while preserving
layout and table structure; it was preferred over lighter extractors because the
multi-column, table-heavy layout of real reports otherwise scrambles the very
reproduction steps the system must read.

\paragraph{The language-model layer.} The heart of the system is Pydantic~AI, a
model-agnostic framework for building tool-using language-model agents with typed,
validated outputs. Two of its properties map directly onto the design. Its provider
abstraction lets the identical extraction and retest code run against the hosted
Claude API or a locally served model with only a configuration change --- which both
keeps sensitive reports on-premises when required and provides the local-versus-cloud
comparison the evaluation uses. Its structured-output support forces a model's
response to conform to a Pydantic schema, which is exactly the schema-constrained,
validated generation that Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} argued is needed to stop a
hallucinated answer from becoming a verdict; its in-memory test models also let the
agent be unit-tested without a network call. The primary reasoning engine is
Anthropic's Claude family, used for its instruction-following and tool-use
reliability across the multi-step retest loop; the local alternative is Ollama
serving open-weight models of the \texttt{qwen} family through an OpenAI-compatible
API, included both as a privacy-preserving deployment option and as a comparison
condition. The switch is made from the interface at runtime, and \texttt{httpx}, a
modern asynchronous HTTP client, backs it: before a provider is committed to, it
queries that provider's model list, so an unreachable backend or a bad key surfaces
as an error in the settings view rather than as a failed extraction later.

\paragraph{Sandboxed execution.} Every command the retest agent is approved to run
executes inside an ephemeral Docker container, driven from the backend through the
\texttt{docker} SDK. Containerisation is how the safety argument is \emph{enforced}
rather than merely stated, and the mechanism is worth being precise about, because
it is easy to overclaim. Each retest session creates its own Docker network in
\texttt{internal} mode --- a network for which the daemon installs no gateway ---
and attaches exactly two members to it: the authorised target and the container the
agent works in. Because the sandbox has no route off that network, anything the
agent may need must already be present, so the image it runs is a curated
penetration-testing toolbox built locally rather than pulled.

Authorisation is therefore \emph{network membership} rather than a list of
permitted addresses checked before each command, and the distinction is the point.
An earlier version of this project did enforce such a list; a check of that kind is
a promise some code path must remember to keep, and it can be defeated by any
address its author did not anticipate. The route the check was meant to forbid is
now simply absent. The agent may compose any command it likes --- a request to an
arbitrary address, a port scan of a private range, a proxy --- and no connection it
opens has anywhere to go.

Three limits of that boundary are stated here rather than left implicit, because a
guarantee a reader cannot check is worth little. First, the lock confines \emph{the
agent}; it does not confine code the agent successfully executes \emph{on the
target}, which retains its own connectivity, since attaching the internal network
does not detach the target from the one it was started on --- a distinction that
matters exactly when a retest of a remote-code-execution finding succeeds. Second,
\texttt{internal} mode blocks routed traffic but not name resolution: where the host
uses a loopback DNS stub --- the default on Debian and Ubuntu --- the daemon's
embedded resolver still forwards external queries from its own namespace, leaving a
low-bandwidth covert channel out of an otherwise TCP-isolated container. Third, the
automated test that asserts the lock proves less than it appears to: it checks only
that a request to an external name fails, which a failed lookup alone satisfies, and
never attempts a bare address. That third limit is an instance of the lesson
Section~\ref{sec:ci} closes on --- a check verifies only what it is aimed at --- here
applied to the safety claim itself. All three are recorded in ADR-0025 alongside the
guarantee.

\paragraph{Front end.} The interface is a single-page application built with
React~19, a component-based UI library, and written in TypeScript, so the type
discipline of the backend continues into the browser. Vite is the build tool and
development server, chosen for its near-instant hot reload and the optimised
production bundle that FastAPI then serves; Tailwind~CSS~4 is the utility-first
styling system used to build the application's bespoke interface without maintaining
a parallel stylesheet. TanStack React~Query manages server state on the client ---
fetching, caching and synchronising the backend's data --- so the interface stays
consistent with the API without hand-written loading and error handling, and React
Router handles navigation between views. The live command-and-output stream of a
gated retest is rendered by xterm.js, a terminal-emulator component that gives the
operator a faithful view of what the agent runs; the interface typefaces (IBM~Plex)
are self-hosted to avoid a third-party font dependency.

\subsection{Quality assurance}
\label{subsec:qa}

Quality is enforced mechanically rather than by convention, and behind each gate is
a specific tool.

\paragraph{Static analysis and style.} \texttt{ruff} is a very fast Rust-based linter
and formatter that consolidates the roles of many older tools; here it enforces
formatting, import order, code modernisation, common-bug patterns, the \texttt{bandit}
security rules (pertinent for a security tool), a complexity ceiling and Google-style
docstrings --- the last because those docstrings feed the generated API documentation
--- in a single pass. \texttt{mypy} is the static type checker, run in
\texttt{-{}-strict} mode so that every function is fully typed and type errors are
caught before runtime. \texttt{xenon}, over the \texttt{radon} metrics library, fails
the build whenever a function exceeds the agreed cyclomatic-complexity ceiling ---
what makes the ``refactor, don't suppress'' rule mechanical rather than aspirational.
Three secondary tools --- \texttt{vulture} for dead code, \texttt{pylint} for deeper
analysis and \texttt{pydeps} for import-dependency graphs --- feed the pre-milestone
\emph{codebase-sanity} audit against the duplication and drift that AI-assisted
development tends to accumulate, and \texttt{pre-commit} runs the quickest checks as a
git hook so problems are caught before they are committed.

\paragraph{Testing.} The backend suite runs on \texttt{pytest} with \texttt{pytest-cov}
measuring coverage, which the CI gate holds at or above 80\% of \texttt{src/}. Tests
follow the classic pyramid: fast unit tests with no input/output (language-model
calls stubbed with Pydantic~AI's test models), integration tests that wire real
components together, and a small number of dockerised system tests against the lab
targets. On the front end, Vitest --- the Vite-native test runner, with the
\texttt{v8} coverage provider --- drives component tests written against a
\texttt{jsdom} in-memory DOM with Testing~Library, which queries components the way a
user would, while ESLint enforces JavaScript/TypeScript quality and React-hooks
correctness.

\subsection{The AI development environment}
\label{subsec:ai-env}

Beyond the application stack, the project was built inside a bespoke development
environment centred on Claude~Code, Anthropic's agentic command-line assistant.
Repeated tasks are encoded as nine project-specific \emph{skills} (for example,
creating an ADR, adding a requirement, opening a board-ready issue, or building the
thesis), and quality checks as three \emph{agents} (a whole-repository sanity
auditor, a documentation curator and a thesis reviewer); three \emph{hooks} automate
governance directly in the editing loop --- one reminds the developer to open a board
issue before coding, one formats code on every edit, and one logs each assistant
session to the public audit trail. The assistant is extended with enabled plugins,
among them GitHub integration, up-to-date library documentation, Pydantic~AI support
and Semgrep --- a static application-security-testing scanner that flags vulnerable
code patterns as they are written, appropriate diligence for a tool that is itself
about security. What the assistant produces in that environment is then checked by
the automated gate described in Section~\ref{sec:ci}.

\subsection{Documentation as code}
\label{subsec:docs}

The project treats documentation as code: nothing that can be generated is
maintained by hand. MkDocs (with the Material theme) is the static-site generator
that assembles the documentation from Markdown, run in a strict mode that fails the
build on any broken cross-reference. Into it, \texttt{mkdocstrings} pulls the API
reference straight from the source docstrings and \texttt{pyreverse} generates the
UML class and package diagrams from the code itself, so neither can drift from the
implementation. The architecture (a C4 model) and the sequence diagrams are authored
as Mermaid --- a text-based diagramming language --- embedded in the Markdown and
rendered by a Mermaid plugin; by rule they are updated in the same change as the code
they describe. A small supporting role falls to \texttt{reportlab}, a PDF-generation
library the test-fixture generator uses to synthesise sample report PDFs for the
suite. Documentation is a first-class
part of the Definition of Done: a change is complete only when its docstrings,
generated pages and affected diagrams are updated together, and the
\emph{doc-curator} agent audits for drift as part of review.

\section{Continuous integration: the automated quality gate}
\label{sec:ci}

The tools of Section~\ref{subsec:qa} are only as good as the discipline with which
they are run, and a developer working alone --- with an assistant that writes most of
the code --- cannot rely on running them by hand. This project therefore follows the
classical continuous-integration practice \cite{fowler_ci, humble_farley}: every
change is verified automatically, on a build machine, against a reproducible
environment, and the verification is what authorises the merge. The motivation is
sharper here than in a conventional project. There is no second developer to review a
diff, and machine-written code fails in a characteristic way --- it is syntactically
clean, idiomatic and plausible on reading, while being wrong about a type, an edge
case or a dependency. A gate that executes the code is a far better detector of that
failure mode than a careful read, and it is the mechanism by which the author's
review authority stays practical instead of nominal.

The automation runs on GitHub~Actions, the repository's built-in continuous-integration
service, through the seven workflows summarised in Table~\ref{tab:workflows}. They fall
into three groups by the role each plays: the per-change gates that authorise a merge,
the scheduled audits that catch what no single change can reveal, and the workflows
that keep the project's published artefacts, and its record of work, in step with the
code.

\paragraph{The per-change gate.} The CI workflow runs six independent jobs: static
quality (style, strict types, the complexity ceiling), unit tests with the coverage
floor, integration tests, the complete front-end pipeline of lint, type check,
production build and component tests, a headless run of every offline demonstration,
and the documentation build. Because the jobs are separate, a failure is
attributed to a specific concern rather than to ``the build''; because they run in
parallel, the whole gate completes in about the time of its slowest job. Two further
details make the result trustworthy. The environment is reproducible --- dependencies
are installed from the lockfile with \texttt{uv sync -{}-locked} and \texttt{npm ci},
so the build machine resolves exactly the versions the developer used, and a green run
means the same thing tomorrow as today. The gate is also enforced at the point of
integration rather than by convention: which checks are \emph{required} is a recorded
decision (ADR-0003) configured in the repository's branch protection, and auto-merge
releases a pull request only when all of them report success --- which is what turns
``the tests should pass'' into ``unverified code cannot reach \texttt{main}''. A run
superseded by a new push is cancelled automatically, keeping feedback fast enough to
stay inside the developer's working loop.

The gate is not a formality, and it is cheap. At the time of writing it had executed
251~CI runs and 257~security runs over the 105~pull requests merged into
\texttt{main}, with a median wall-clock time of 37~seconds and a worst case under
seven minutes. Sixteen CI runs and ten security runs ended red. Fifteen of those were
on a pull request --- a change stopped before it reached the main branch --- and the
rest on a push to \texttt{main} or on the weekly schedule, catching what had already
landed or what a newly published advisory had just made unsafe.

\paragraph{Security scanning in depth.} A tool that runs commands against live
systems, handles API credentials and parses untrusted files must hold itself to the
standard it is built to check, so security scanning is a merge gate rather than an
occasional exercise, and it is deliberately layered: each of its four jobs sees a
class of defect the others are blind to. \texttt{pip-audit} resolves the project's
locked dependency set and matches it against published advisories, covering the
supply chain --- the code the project did not write but does ship --- while
Dependabot proposes the corresponding upgrade weekly for the Python, front-end and
workflow dependency sets. Bandit performs pattern-based
static application-security testing over the application and its supporting
scripts, reporting findings of medium
severity and above: insecure temporary files, disabled certificate verification, weak
cryptographic primitives. The severity floor is deliberate, since a tool that must
legitimately spawn processes would otherwise drown in low-severity notices about the
very behaviour it exists to perform. CodeQL \cite{codeql} goes further by querying a
semantic model of the program, tracing whether data from an untrusted source reaches a
sensitive sink --- reasoning that pattern matching cannot perform. It analyses the
Python sources and the TypeScript front end alike; that second language was added
late, after the audit discussed at the end of this section observed that the browser
code was covered by its type checker and linter but by no data-flow analysis, and
that its twenty-eight dependencies were reaching no scanner at all. Gitleaks scans
the \emph{entire history} rather than the current tree, because
deleting a leaked key in a later commit does not remove it: the secret remains
retrievable from any clone, and the correct response is to find it and revoke it.

This workflow also runs on a weekly schedule, and not only on change. Dependency risk
is not change-triggered: a dependency that was clean when merged becomes vulnerable
the day an advisory is published, with nobody having touched the code. A purely
event-driven gate would therefore certify a project as secure precisely while it is
quietly ageing into vulnerability.

\paragraph{Slow and longitudinal checks, off the critical path.} Not every valuable
check belongs in the merge gate. The system tests bring up the dockerised lab
targets, wait for them to become healthy, run the end-to-end suite against them and
tear them down unconditionally; they are slow and depend on external images, and
making them blocking would mean that an unrelated container hiccup stops all work ---
the reliable way to end up disabling a test suite. Run nightly instead, they still
surface a regression within a day, at no cost to flow. The weekly sanity-metrics
workflow serves a different purpose again: it collects complexity, dead-code and
duplication measurements into a report artefact that feeds the pre-milestone audit.
These are \emph{longitudinal} signals --- the slow accumulation of duplicated helpers
and drifting patterns that AI-assisted development is prone to --- and by
construction no review of a single diff can see them.

\paragraph{Publication and traceability.} The remaining three workflows keep the
project's artefacts, and its record of work, from falling behind the code. The
documentation workflow publishes to GitHub~Pages on every merge, the strict build
that fails on any broken cross-reference having already run before the merge as part
of the gate; the thesis workflow builds this memoir with XeLaTeX whenever
\texttt{thesis/} changes, so the document is known to compile continuously rather than
on the eve of deposit; and the board workflow moves the Kanban card in response to
repository events --- an issue opened lands in Backlog, a pull request that declares
\texttt{Closes~\#n} moves its issue to In~Progress, a green CI run moves it to Verify,
and closing the issue moves it to Done. That last workflow is what makes the flow
discipline of Section~\ref{sec:process} sustainable for one person: the board reflects
reality because it is derived from repository events, not from manual bookkeeping that
a busy developer would skip. The one transition left manual is \emph{Validate}, which
the author performs only after personally running the pull request's validation steps.

\paragraph{What the gate proves, and what it does not.} It is worth being precise
about the limits of this apparatus, because automation invites overclaiming. A green
build establishes mechanical properties: the code is typed and formatted, no function
exceeds the agreed complexity ceiling, the unit suite passes with at least 80\% line
coverage and the integration suite passes, no locked dependency carries a published
advisory, no secret appears anywhere in the history, and the front end compiles and
satisfies its component tests. It establishes none of the properties that matter most
to a security tool --- that a verdict about a vulnerability is correct, that a retest
actually exercised the reported flaw, that the agent's reasoning is sound. Those are
empirical questions about the system's outputs, answered by evaluation against ground
truth rather than by the build. The gate's contribution is narrower but still
essential: when such an evaluation is run, it is run on code whose mechanical
soundness is not in doubt.

The scanners also impose a real cost that should be stated honestly, because a gate
of this kind is usually described only by its benefits. They produce false positives,
and they do so with the authority of a blocking check. In this project, CodeQL's
\texttt{incomplete-url-substring-sanitization} rule fired on a \emph{test} assertion
that identified a command by substring, and blocked pull request~\#89 until the
assertion was rewritten as an exact command-contract check --- a better test, as it
happens, but the improvement was incidental to the alert. The policy adopted is to
treat every finding as blocking and to resolve it either by changing the code or by
recording an explicit, justified dismissal, never by lowering the threshold, which
would quietly hollow out the gate while leaving it green.

A subtler failure mode emerged late in the project and deserves recording, because
it is invisible from inside the practice that produces it. A gate verifies only what
it is aimed at, and the boundary of what it is aimed at is itself a decision that
nobody revisits once made. An audit the author ran across the whole repository found
that \texttt{scripts/} --- 1{,}076 lines, of which 641 are the demonstration
programs --- sat outside every check: outside the type checker, the linter and the
security scanner, and outside the test suite. That directory is not incidental. The
project's own Definition of Done makes a runnable demonstration the mandatory
evidence that a change works, so those programs are the mechanism by which every
other claim is validated. Two of them had stopped working and the author had not
noticed, and one had stopped for an instructive reason: a change to how the agent
executes a turn altered a contract the demonstration depended on, the failure was
absorbed by an error-handling boundary that recorded it as a session state rather
than raising it, and no gate was watching that file. The verification apparatus was,
in that narrow sense, verifying itself less rigorously than it verified the
application.

The general lesson is that a coverage figure measures how much of the code a gate
exercises, not how much of the repository the gate is aimed at. A green build says
nothing about the code the build never compiles, and the natural place for such a
blind spot to open is exactly where a project keeps its supporting machinery ---
scripts, hooks, generators, fixtures --- which feels peripheral while being
load-bearing. The response was to widen the perimeter rather than to add another
check inside it: the scripts were brought under the type checker, the linter and the
security scanner, and a job was added that executes every offline demonstration on
every change, so that the evidence a reviewer is told to trust is itself
continuously verified. In this project, then, the measure of the gate turned out to
be not only its strictness but its reach.

\section{Use of Artificial Intelligence Tools}
\label{sec:ai-declaration}

In compliance with §6 of the ESII TFG regulation, this section declares the use of
artificial-intelligence tools in the production of this work. It is compiled from
the project's public audit trail: the curated usage log under
\texttt{docs/ai-usage/} --- maintained in real time through the early milestones and
completed retrospectively for the later ones from the automatically recorded session
files and the git history --- together with the \texttt{Co-Authored-By: Claude}
trailers in that history. The large majority of the project's commits carry the
trailer; the remainder are author-only commits, and, because the project integrates by
squash-merge, each merge commit inherits the trailers of the assistant-authored work
it absorbs.

\paragraph{Tools used.} The single AI tool used for development was Claude Code,
Anthropic's agentic command-line assistant, driven by Anthropic's Claude family of
models; the specific model versions used in the earliest milestones are not recorded
in the project's audit trail, while the later milestones and the assistance with this
dissertation used the Opus~4 family. Local open-weight models (Ollama, \texttt{qwen}
family) were used only \emph{as a component and comparison condition of the system
under study}, never to author the thesis or the code.

\paragraph{Type of use.} The assistance spanned five categories, in every case under
the author's direction and subject to the author's review:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Research and analysis} --- reading the regulations and the proposal and
  surveying related work.
\item \emph{Code generation} --- implementing the backend (\texttt{src/revalid}) and
  the React front end (\texttt{frontend/src}) from the author's specifications.
\item \emph{Test writing} --- unit, integration and system tests across the pyramid.
\item \emph{Documentation generation} --- API documentation, the C4 architecture
  and sequence diagrams, and the project roadmap.
\item \emph{Thesis writing assistance} --- drafting and editing chapters, including
  verifying that every cited reference resolves to a real source.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Affected areas.} In the repository, assistance touched the backend and
front-end source, the test suites, the ADRs and requirements, the documentation and
CI configuration, and the assistant's own skills, agents and hooks. In this
dissertation, the chapters listed above were drafted with assistance; all were
reviewed, corrected and approved by the author.

\paragraph{Authorship statement.} All design, architecture and scope decisions were
made by the author, as evidenced by the thirty-eight ADRs and the requirements
specification that precede the code. Every AI output --- code, tests, documentation
and thesis prose --- was reviewed and validated by the author before acceptance,
through pull-request validation and the continuous-integration gate. The author is
the sole author of, and is responsible for, this work; the AI tool served as an
assistant that accelerated implementation, not as a substitute for the author's
